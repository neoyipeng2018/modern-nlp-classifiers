Slice shape

Every slice ends with a public model, a public dataset, and numbers in the registry. A slice that ends with only code is not finished.

How the cuts were made

Slicing rules

One task per slice

What every slice must do

  • Go end to end. Raw text, labels, splits, training, evaluation, release.
  • End with something published, not something ready to publish.
  • Pass every gate in Program design before the next slice starts.
  • Leave the shared harness usable by the next slice without modification, or record what had to change and why.

What no slice may do

  • Build machinery for a later slice on the grounds that it will be needed. It will be needed differently.
  • Start before the previous slice's model is released.
  • Adopt a borrowed benchmark, or a frontier reference, without clearing Gate 1 on its licence, its label definitions and its overlap with training data.
  • Ship a number without a registry row.

Slice 1 is roughly half the project. That is not a planning error. Choosing one slice per task means the shared harness has to live inside the first task rather than in a separate foundation phase. The cost is a long first slice. The benefit is that the harness gets designed against a real task instead of an imagined one.

The long one

Slice 1 — Forward-looking, and the harness

Start here

Slice 1

Ship modernfin-fls, and everything the other two slices reuse

Not started

Forward-looking goes first because it has the strongest borrowed benchmark of the three, and because its sentences come from 10-K filings that are on EDGAR in full. That makes it the task most likely to get its passages back, which is what the long-context claim now rests on. The risk stays concentrated in the machinery, where you can see it, rather than in the task definition, where it hides.

New in this slice

  • Everything. Data schema, ingest, passage builder, splits, input builder, training loop, evaluation harness, registry, baselines, cards, CI.
  • The first codebook, transcribed from somebody else's three class definitions, plus the passage reconstructor and the context-sensitivity probe that stand in for the evidence-distance field.
  • Both benchmarks: the reference set labelled by gpt-5.6-sol, and the borrowed human referee set, split and hashed apart.
  • The whole weak-supervision pipeline: router client, labelling functions, label matrix, label model.
  • The serverless training job, its cost accounting, and the per-rung cost of both training and labelling.
  • The truncation gate, the backbone bench across four encoder families, and the first length ladder.
  • The optional CPU inference bench. Nothing waits on it, and dropping it is a normal outcome.

Deliberately deferred

  • The aspect field. Schema has a place for it, and nothing uses it yet.
  • The demo Space.
  • Any cross-task comparison.
  • Tuning the labelling-function set beyond what Gate 4 needs. Two of the three tasks have not been priced yet.

Done when

  • modernfin-fls is public with a complete card.
  • The headline is fidelity: agreement with the pinned reference, stated as such, with the cost ratio and latency beside it.
  • The weak-to-strong pair is published: student agreement against teacher-ensemble agreement, on the same frozen set.
  • The match-or-exceed comparison is reported on the referee set, or its absence is explained.
  • Both benchmarks are documented: the reference model identifier with its cached labels, and the referee set's source, licence, published agreement or lack of one, and reconstruction match rate.
  • The reconstruction artifact is public as identifiers and offsets, so anyone can rebuild the same passages without us mirroring someone else's labels.
  • Every baseline number is in one comparison table, paired against the ensemble.
  • The length ladder is published, including the rungs that lost and the truncation rate at each.
  • The backbone bench is published, all nine candidates at 512, including the ones that lost.
  • CPU latency is published wherever it was measured, with the machine, the thread count, the export path and the macro-F1 of that export. Where it was not measured, that is stated and no CPU claim is made.
  • Labelling-function diagnostics are published per rung, including pairwise agreement.
  • The hardware table has real numbers in it, including cost per rung.
  • Labelling spend, training spend and inference cost are all measured per rung, not estimated.
  • A clean checkout reproduces the headline table.
  • The harness is ready for Slice 2 with no known task-specific hacks.

Risks specific to this slice

  • The labelling bill is the new largest risk. Buying labels at three rungs, across k models, over 8,192-token passages, is the biggest line in the budget and nobody has priced it yet. Step 10 exists to find out before step 12 commits.
  • The bench may not separate the top few. Nine similar-sized modern encoders on one task can easily land inside each other's confidence intervals. That is a result, not a failure. The CPU tie-break picks the model and the card says so. Do not add seeds hunting for a gap that is not there.
  • NeoBERT is the awkward one. Custom modelling code, so trust_remote_code, a pinned revision, its own LoRA target modules, and a 4,096 ceiling that fits none of the three rungs. Step 2 finds this out, not step 17.
  • The routed models may agree too much. Gate 4 catches it. The fallback is majority vote, more heuristic functions, or a more diverse model list.
  • A model in the set may not really read 8,192 tokens. Silent truncation on the teacher side would poison the top rung specifically, which is the rung the whole project is about. Check context handling per model during the pilot.
  • Reconstruction is the single point of failure. The benchmark ships bare sentences. If they cannot be matched back into their filings, this slice has no long-context result at all, only a cost result. Measure the match rate at step 5, before anything is trained, and treat 60% as the line.
  • The reference may have read the referee benchmark. It has been on Hugging Face for years, and the match-or-exceed claim is exactly the one it would inflate. Score the reference on fresh unlabelled EDGAR passages as well and publish the gap.
  • The ceiling may be under the bar, and there are two of them. The reference's agreement with itself across the three key passes caps everything, and it is known at step 6b. The teacher ensemble's agreement with the key caps the student, and it is known at step 10. Both land before the training bill. If either is below 95%, say so and move the bar rather than chasing it.
  • The task may be largely lexical. If TF-IDF gets close to the encoder, that is a genuine finding, and Gate 5 exists to make you stop and check rather than tune past it. Forward-looking is the most likely of the three to be decidable from the sentence alone, which also makes it the task least likely to reward long context.
  • Three classes, not two. The borrowed set separates specific from non-specific forward-looking statements. The product spec's binary definition is gone, and every downstream claim, prompt and labelling function has to be rewritten for three classes before step 9.
Exit gate: Gates 1 through 7 all pass. Slice 2 does not start until the FLS model is public. If Gate 6 fails, the model still ships with the miss recorded, and Slice 2 still starts. A length ladder that comes out flat is not a gate failure, it is the result.

One new thing on a proven pipeline

Slice 2 — Aspect-based sentiment

Second

Slice 2

Ship modernfin-absa

Not started

Second because it changes exactly one part of a pipeline that already works: the aspect goes into the input as a first segment. Everything else is Slice 1's code running on different data.

New in this slice

  • The aspect field, in the schema, the input builder and the library surface.
  • Three classes instead of two, which changes the metric breakdown and the confusion reporting.
  • A documented mapping from continuous sentiment scores in public sources to the three classes.
  • Multiple examples per sentence, one per aspect, which the leakage check must understand.

Reused unchanged

  • Ingest, splitting, dedup, provenance.
  • Training loop, checkpointing, resume.
  • Evaluation harness, registry, report cards, release scripts.
  • Baseline runners and the router client, with a new prompt and new few-shot examples.
  • The weak-supervision pipeline, including the per-rung pilot rule.
  • The passage builder and the truncation gate.
  • The length ladder, rerun for this task under the shortening rule in Program design.

Work in order

#StepWhat it produces
1Benchmark adoption and Gate 1Pull pauri32/fiqa-2018, the 1,213-row FiQA 2018 Task 1 set. Every row carries a target company, an aspect from a published taxonomy, a continuous score from −1 to 1, a mapped class, and the snippet the score was read from. Confirm terms, quote any published agreement figure or state that none exists.
2Score mapping decisionThresholds turning the continuous scores into positive, negative and neutral, decided on the development split and written down before use. FiQA ships its own mapped class, so the choice is now between adopting the source mapping and justifying a different one. Either way it is a documented decision, because it materially shapes the results.
3Codebook v1The ABSA codebook rewritten to describe what FiQA's annotators actually did, with the hard cases from the product spec checked against real rows. Where our rule and theirs disagree, theirs wins and the difference is recorded. No pilot rounds, because there is nobody to pilot.
4Aspect vocabularySettled by adoption. Aspects come from FiQA's taxonomy, so the open "aspect vocabulary" question in the product spec closes here. Record the taxonomy, its depth, and how sparse the tail is, because a taxonomy with many one-example aspects is a different problem from a flat one.
5Re-splitFiQA's official test split is 150 rows, which is too small to separate anything. Pool all 1,213, cut 500 for test and 200 for development, grouped by target company so the same firm cannot straddle. Score the official 150-row split as well, so published comparisons stay possible. Count the multi-aspect sentences that survive into test, because they are what the diagnostic in step 10 needs.
6Freeze, hash, split checksSame as Slice 1, with the added rule that all aspects of one sentence stay in the same split. There is no reconstruction step here. FiQA is tweets and headlines, so there is no surrounding document and never was.
7Input builder extensionAspect as first segment, with tests for an aspect that contains marker-like text and an aspect absent from the passage.
8Labelling, pilot firstThe aspect goes into the labelling prompt too. Pilot per rung, Gate 4, then the full runs. Expect lower agreement between models here than on the binary tasks, and expect the label model to matter more because of it.
9Baselines and trainingSame runners, new prompts. The backbone is inherited from Slice 1 and the bench does not re-run. Then the length ladder, three seeds per rung, plus the architecture control, and the CPU latency numbers if that optional step ran in Slice 1.
10Multi-aspect diagnosticA held-out set of sentences carrying two aspects with opposite labels, scored separately. This is the evidence that the model uses the aspect rather than the sentence, and it is the honest version of the entity claim the previous project could not support.
11Final evaluation and releaseAs Slice 1.

The multi-aspect diagnostic is the interesting result in this slice. A model can score well on aspect sentiment by ignoring the aspect entirely and reading overall sentence tone. The only way to know is to test sentences where the aspects disagree. If accuracy collapses there, the model is doing sentence sentiment with extra steps, and the card must say so. Run it before writing any claim about aspect awareness.

Done when

  • modernfin-absa is public with a complete card.
  • The benchmark is documented, and the re-split is published as row identifiers so anyone can rebuild it.
  • The score mapping is published, including whether it matches FiQA's own.
  • The card states plainly that this task ships sentence-level, with a cost ladder rather than an accuracy ladder.
  • The multi-aspect diagnostic result is reported, whatever it says.
  • Clean-checkout reproduction passes.

Risks specific to this slice

  • Score thresholds decide the result. Different mappings give different headline numbers. Pick once, on validation, and publish the choice.
  • The neutral class is the hardest. It absorbs everything ambiguous, and it will dominate the confusion matrix. Report per-class numbers, not just macro-F1.
  • The benchmark is microblogs and headlines, which is a domain shift from the filings the other two models see. This is now the test set, not an out-of-domain check, so the card cannot claim filing-level ABSA performance from it.
  • No long-context claim is available here at all. Tweets have no surrounding document. The ladder measures cost and speed, and any accuracy difference between rungs on 500 sentence-length rows is noise. Say that up front rather than reporting a flat ladder as a finding.
  • 500 test rows across three classes is a small benchmark. Publish intervals, and call a rung comparison inconclusive when they overlap.
  • The model may ignore the aspect. Caught by the diagnostic, not by the headline metric.
Exit gate: Gates 1 through 7, plus the multi-aspect diagnostic run and reported. A missing diagnostic blocks release, because without it the model card cannot honestly describe what the model does.

Weakest data, strongest pipeline

Slice 3 — Fact versus opinion

Third

Slice 3

Ship modernfin-factop

Not started

Last because there is little usable public finance data for it. That makes it the slice that leans hardest on weak supervision, so it runs when the pipeline is proven and its cost at passage length is already known.

New in this slice

  • Task-specific labelling functions, where the heuristics are hardest to write and the models disagree most.
  • The largest labelling run of the three, since there is almost no public data to lean on.
  • The fork, which is the only decision in the programme that changes what a model is called.
  • The with and without weak-supervision ablation, reported as two results.
  • The overlap analysis against the forward-looking labels.

This is the task where long context should matter most. "Margins will improve" is opinion, and the same sentence under a speaker tag is a reported fact. In a transcript that tag can be a long way back. If the ladder is flat here, it is unlikely to help anywhere.

Reused unchanged

  • Everything from Slices 1 and 2 except the aspect field.
  • The binary metric setup from Slice 1.
  • The whole release path.

Work in order

#StepWhat it produces
0Resolve the forkThe blocking decision for this slice, and the only one in the programme that changes a product. Either the task becomes premise versus claim and adopts ChanceFocus/flare-finarg-ecc-auc, or it stays fact versus opinion and ships with no accuracy claim. Written down, with the reason, before any other step. Recommendation is the rename.
1aIf renamed: adopt and split969 earnings-call sentences labelled premise or claim by people, one split as shipped. Cut 600 for test and 200 for development, grouped by call. Attempt passage reconstruction against public transcript sources and expect a lower match rate than FLS. The codebook is rewritten to FinArg's definitions, and the model is renamed to match.
1bIf not renamed: no refereeThe codebook keeps the checkability test, the attribution rule and the mixed-sentence rule. The headline is unaffected, because it was never the human set. What is lost is the referee, so this task can report fidelity, cost and latency but can never claim to match or exceed the frontier model. FinArg is still scored as a related-task transfer check, clearly labelled as a different label definition.
3Labelling functionsThe task prompt, plus heuristics for attribution patterns, evaluative adjectives and the presence of a figure with a unit. Attribution heuristics have to look back through the passage, not just the sentence, so they are the hardest of the three tasks to write.
4Pilot per rung, then the full runs500 rows at each rung for cost and quality, Gate 4, then labels at volume. Expect the largest labelling spend of the three tasks, because there is least public data to substitute for it.

6Baselines and final evaluationAs before.

Watch the circularity here more than anywhere. Nearly every training label comes from the same models the student is measured against. Under branch 1a the human test set, the ensemble comparison and the two-track reporting are what keep the result meaningful. Under branch 1b the human test set is gone, so only two of those three defences remain, and the write-up has to say so in the same paragraph as the result.

Done when

  • modernfin-factop is public with a complete card.
  • Both training tracks are published, with and without the weakly supervised rows.
  • The fork is resolved in writing, and the card names which branch was taken.
  • Label model accuracy against the development set is measured and stated, per rung. Under branch 1b that development set is model-labelled, and the number is graded accordingly.
  • The overlap with forward-looking is reported.
  • Clean-checkout reproduction passes.

Risks specific to this slice

  • The two labels are not the same. FinArg's own examples show it. A sentence opening "I think a lot is happening" is labelled a premise there, and would be opinion under our codebook. Renaming the task is honest. Quietly treating premise as fact would not be.
  • The weakly supervised rows may not help. That is a publishable ablation result, and it is a better outcome than an unproven claim.
  • The labels may overlap heavily with forward-looking. If most forward-looking sentences are also opinion, part of the thesis needs restating. Report it either way.
  • Branch 1b has no floor. Without a human test set there is nothing to catch the ensemble being confidently wrong in a consistent way, and the student would inherit it invisibly. That is the real cost of keeping the original definition, and it is why the recommendation is the rename.
Exit gate: Gates 1 through 7, plus both training tracks reported and the label model accuracy measured.

Three projects become one claim

Slice 4 — Suite release

Last

Slice 4

The write-up, the demo, and the thesis test

Not started

Three released models is three separate results. This slice is what turns them into an argument, and it is also where the argument gets tested rather than asserted.

What gets built

  • The Triad library surface, loading all three models with one call, plus scan for whole documents.
  • The demo Space, showing all three outputs, the passage the model actually read, and a toggle to rerun the same input through the 512-token control.
  • The cross-model comparison table, generated from report cards.
  • The cross-task length section: three ladders side by side, and an honest answer to whether long context earned its cost.
  • The public write-up, following the fixed structure in Program design.

The thesis test

  • Take a set of real finance NLP tasks. Screening transcripts for guidance changes, ranking analyst notes, filtering RAG chunks, and so on.
  • For each, say what the three labels give you and what they do not.
  • Publish the gaps at the same length as the coverage.
  • If the thesis does not hold, say what a fourth classifier would have to do.

Done when

  • The write-up is public with every number graded and traceable.
  • The demo is live and anyone can paste text into it.
  • The README reproduces the headline table in one command.
  • The quarantine section lists claims that were tested and dropped.
  • The thesis section reports coverage and gaps honestly.

Risks specific to this slice

  • Numbers drift between documents. Prevented by generating every table from report cards rather than typing them.
  • The write-up quietly softens a negative result. Prevented by writing the protocol section before looking at the results section.
  • The demo becomes a maintenance obligation. Keep it small, and say in the README that it is a demo with no uptime promise.

The reasoning behind the sequence

Why this order

Assumed
TaskLabel difficultyPublic dataNew machineryPosition
Forward-looking Lowest Some All of it First. Strongest borrowed benchmark, and the only one whose sentences come from documents that can be fetched in full, so it is where reconstruction has the best chance.
Aspect sentiment Medium Most One thing Second. Adds exactly one new mechanism to a working pipeline. Its benchmark is the smallest, and it has no long-context story at all.
Fact vs opinion Highest Least Hardest heuristics Third. The only task with no matching public benchmark, so it carries the fork. Doing it last means the labelling pipeline is proven and priced before that call has to be made.

The alternative that was rejected. Starting with aspect sentiment, because it has the best public data and continues the previous work most directly. Rejected because it would put the hardest input format and the new machinery in the same slice, and when something failed you would not know which one caused it.

Concretely, next

Where to start

Slice 1, steps 1 to 3

Three things unblock everything else, and two of them can run at the same time. None of them needs a decision that has not already been made.

Do1

Answer the open questions

Two of them are already closed. There is no annotator question, because nobody annotates. ABSA aspects come from FiQA's taxonomy. What is left is the licence the repo will carry now that reconstructed passages rather than sentences are being published, the labelling budget ceiling with the router and the k models behind it, and the fact-versus-opinion fork. The reference is settled: gpt-5.6-sol at medium effort. The fork can wait for Phase 3. The other two gate the first real spend.

Output: a decision-log entry each in Product.
Do2

Stand up the serverless job and price every rung

A pinned image, a volume, and a few hundred steps of ModernBERT-base at 512, then 2,048, then 8,192, in bf16. Record peak memory, minutes and dollars per rung, and confirm resume works. Load all nine bench checkpoints in the same job, so the loading surprises land on day one. Then run the 500-row labelling pilot at each rung as soon as the router client exists, because labelling is the larger of the two bills and nobody has priced it.

Output: the cost table in Architecture filled in with real numbers, training and labelling, for all three rungs.
Do3

Prove the reconstruction works

Take 200 rows from FinanceMTEB/FLS and try to match each sentence back into a 10-K on EDGAR. Record the match rate and the method that matched each row. This is the cheapest possible test of the one assumption the whole long-context claim now rests on, and it can run before any other code exists. Separately, trace the dataset's terms back to the original annotation effort, because no licence is declared on the hub and a licence read wrong is discovered at release time.

Output: a measured match rate on 200 rows, a written licence finding, and a go or no-go on the long-context claim for Slice 1. If the pilot rate is far below 60%, the programme's headline changes before a line of training code is written, which is exactly when you want to know.

Then write the codebook. Everything after step 3 depends on the label definition being written down. It is no longer written from scratch and pilot-tested. It is transcribed from what FLS's annotators actually did, with every place our old definition disagreed recorded as a difference. Least technical work in the project, highest leverage, and nothing else should start before it.
